Ticket 101 · Tauhid's tee

Good performance · right sequence · more agents than strictly necessary, by design

The team nailed the order of operations: inventory first, then accounting, then customer service.

  • Inventory established the two facts everything else depended on: size S was at 0, and Bulldog Print would not ship while invoice 501 ($840, overdue since 08-28) was open.
  • Accounting priced the fix before asking for it, checking $3,400 of cash against $3,240 of obligations, and filed approval 1.
  • Customer service wrote to Tauhid only after the facts existed. Its final draft gave him a real date (an estimated 2026-09-05) without leaking the invoice or the vendor.
The plan had excluded accounting because, on the pre-reset books, 501 was already paid. On the reset books it wasn't, and boss's own rule, "accounting only if a vendor is actually blocked", fired exactly when it should have.

On architecture. One agent holding every tool would have been simpler to build. One prompt, no delegation plumbing, and probably fewer tokens: session 1 alone used 26 model requests and about 132k input tokens across four agents. But the multi-agent version was easier to audit and easier to solution:

  • Separation of duties is real, not decorative. The agent that asked to pay the vendor (accounting, approval 1) is not the agent that asked to buy from it (inventory, PO 1 / approval 2), and neither could execute either. A single all-powerful agent collapses that control into one prompt.
  • The audit reads like a story. Each note on ticket 101 is signed by the role that wrote it, and the delegation tree (boss → inventory → … → customer service) is reconstructible from audit_trail.json. When something goes wrong, I know which job description to fix.
  • Each role carries less. Per-role tool lists halved the tool schema each agent sees (Token efficiency, harness.md), and specialist prompts stay focused.

The cost showed up as coordination overhead. Ownership of 101 changed hands four times, and the final draft dropped the M/L alternatives the first draft had offered. Both are fixable in the prompts; neither changed the outcome.

Ticket 102 · Rent

Good performance · plan matched exactly

I called it correctly: accounting, and only accounting. Boss made exactly one delegation.

  • Accounting checked the payments ledger, the lease, and a payment preview before filing approval 3 for $2,400. The double-payment guard I worried about in the plan held, because accounting looked for a prior payment before it asked for a new one.
  • After I approved, boss closed the ticket in a 6-request session with zero delegations. It verified payment 2 itself rather than re-summoning accounting to confirm what the ledger already said.
That is the "smallest team that can finish" principle working as written.

The one blemish is forward-looking. Paying September's rent left $160.00 against $2,400.00 due 2026-10-02, a projected −$2,248.00 once the $8 PO is billed (Cash tab, "Looking ahead"). Nobody raised it. Both sessions used a 30-day cash window that ends 09-30, two days short. The plan flagged this exact risk in advance; the agents walked past it. The fix is mechanical: rent checks should always look through the next due date.

The plan's sequence held: inventory first (8 on hand, 12 short, restock $264 against $160 cash), accounting second (20 at list = $1,160.00, 62.1% margin). Customer service came after the decision, exactly where the plan deferred it.

What I didn't anticipate was the hinge between those steps.

  • I expected boss to recommend a 10% discount and accounting to file it. Boss gave no number. Accounting, correctly, refused to invent one. The ticket parked on "waiting on human direction", a state the dashboard had no button for.
  • We added Direction from the Desk mid-run. I ruled no discount for now: the policy isn't set, and the cash can't fund the restock. The team then executed that ruling cleanly, with an honest draft offering 8 now at $464 and 12 later at list, and nothing pulled or ordered until the club confirms.
In the end, performance wasn't affected: the right answer reached the customer draft and not a dollar moved. The lesson is about the interface between agents and the human. An agent that refuses to guess is behaving well only if the human has a way to answer it.

What I'd change next

  • Make cash checks always look through the next rent date, and have accounting raise an alert whenever the projected balance goes negative.
  • Require boss to attach a recommended percentage, with margin, to any discount brief.
  • Pin ownership: one owner per ticket unless boss records a reason to reassign.
  • Have customer service keep in-stock alternatives in every revised draft, and use product names, not SKUs.

Three new problems this team could solve

1 · Bulk-order discount negotiationcan solve

Scenario
Student orgs, teams and reunion classes ask for 20–200 units at a "bulk rate", the generalized version of ticket 103.
Who
Customer service takes the request. Inventory confirms coverage and restock cost. Accounting prices tiers (quote_order at 0 / 10 / 15%) against a margin floor and files request_discount_approval. Boss recommends, and the human approves.
Evidence
Ticket 103's numbers show the room: 10% off still clears 57.9% margin, and 15% clears 55.4%. The real constraint was cash, not margin. A deposit from the club would have funded the $264 restock.
Missing piece
A written discount policy (margin floor, volume tiers, deposit rule) so routine requests don't need a ruling each time, plus a sales ledger so deposits count as cash.

2 · An order that failed to be placedcan solve

Scenario
A restock never actually happens. The vendor is blocked, the PO is refused for cash, it sits in pending_approval, or its ETA passes without receipt. Meanwhile a customer is still waiting.
Who
Inventory sweeps list_purchase_orders and check_vendor_restock for stuck or late orders. Accounting clears the cause (an open invoice, as with 501, or a cash shortfall). Customer service sends the affected customer an honest revised date. Boss escalates anything that needs a signature.
Evidence
Ticket 101 was exactly this failure in miniature. The first attempt to restock was impossible until invoice 501 was paid, and the team diagnosed it, routed it, and recovered within three sessions.
Missing piece
A "late PO" signal (ETA passed and status still placed) surfaced in get_shop_overview, so the sweep happens without anyone asking.

3 · Supply that sales can't absorbcan solve

Scenario
The shop buys more than it sells, and cash ends up on shelves instead of in the bank.
Who
Inventory runs get_demand_signals (slow movers and stockout risks). Accounting converts the flags into dollars tied up and proposes a markdown or a reorder freeze. Boss brings one recommendation to the human; customer service promotes approved markdowns.
Evidence
The shelf today holds $1,024 of stock at cost, 6.4 times the $160 in checking. The 40 Yale caps alone are $240 at cost, more than the bank balance, with no open demand. Meanwhile, size-M hoodies are the stockout risk. The money is in the wrong place: a hoodie restock the shop can't fund sits next to caps nobody has asked for.
Missing piece
A sales ledger. Today movement history starts with the agent team, so "slow" is weak evidence. With real sell-through data the same tools become a reliable merchandising signal.

Three problems this team can't address

1 · Shipping errorsout of reach

Why not
There is no shipments table, no tracking number, and no carrier integration. QuickShip CT exists only as a row in vendors. Rule 6 forbids contacting customers or vendors, so the team can't confirm a delivery, open a carrier claim, or ask a customer what arrived. It would be guessing about the physical world.
What it would take
A shipments and returns data model, read-only carrier tracking, a returns policy, and a human who can contact the carrier. Even then, the team would triage while people resolve.

2 · Distribution optimizationout of reach

Why not
There is one shop (Chapel Street), one checking account, and no demand by location, transport costs, or capacity. More fundamentally, this is an optimization problem: allocation across sites and lanes belongs to a solver, not to a conversation between language models. The team can describe trade-offs; it can't compute optimal flows from data it doesn't have.
What it would take
Multi-site inventory and demand data, cost models, and an optimization tool the agents call and explain, rather than one they imitate.

3 · New product discoveryout of reach

Why not
The catalog is four SKUs, and the database has no market research, trend data, or customer feedback beyond three tickets. The team's charter says "report only what tools returned". That is right for books and stock, and it is exactly why the team won't invent the next best-seller. Discovery requires taste, external signals, and campus knowledge it doesn't have.
What it would take
Sales history, structured customer feedback, and access to external trend sources. Even then, the team's role would be to shortlist and cost ideas (margin, vendor fit, lead time) for a human merchant who chooses.
